Certificate Trust Chain Builder
Every HTTPS connection depends on a chain of trust from a Root CA to the website you're visiting. Three scenarios — arrange certificates in the correct chain order, then answer questions about what breaks trust and why browsers show warnings.
ROOT CA
Self-signed · Pre-installed in OS/browser · Trusted by default
↓ signs
INTERMEDIATE CA
Signed by Root · Signs end-entity certs · Insulates Root CA
↓ signs
END-ENTITY CERT
Signed by Intermediate · Issued to website/server
// KEY CONCEPTS
CHAIN OF TRUST
Each certificate is signed by the one above it. Root CA → Intermediate CA → End-entity cert. Browsers trust Root CAs pre-installed in the OS. If any link in the chain is broken (expired, revoked, self-signed), the browser warns the user.
WHY INTERMEDIATE CAs?
Root CAs are kept offline for security. If an Intermediate CA is compromised, the Root CA can revoke it without affecting the entire trust ecosystem. This separation limits the blast radius of a CA compromise.
CERTIFICATE REVOCATION
CRL (Certificate Revocation List) and OCSP (Online Certificate Status Protocol) allow CAs to mark certificates as untrusted before they expire — e.g., if a private key is compromised. Browsers check revocation status during TLS handshakes.
SELF-SIGNED CERTIFICATES
A self-signed certificate is signed by itself — no CA verifies the identity. Browsers show a warning because there's no third-party trust anchor. Fine for internal/dev use, never acceptable for public-facing sites.
